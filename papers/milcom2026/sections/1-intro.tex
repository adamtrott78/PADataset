Modern military operations no longer unfold in a single domain. Adversaries coordinate effects across land, sea, air, space, cyberspace, and the electromagnetic spectrum (EMS), and the boundaries between these domains are increasingly porous: a cyber intrusion may be enabled by an RF foothold, and an electronic-warfare (EW) action may be the precursor to a later cyber or kinetic effect. Sustaining warfighting overmatch in this environment requires more than passive monitoring; it requires the deliberate engineering of Quantifiable and Resilient Cyberspace Windows of Superiority (QR-CWoS). These are bounded intervals during which defenders can act with information and timing advantage over an adversary, and during which that advantage can be measured, predicted, and reproduced. A QR-CWoS is only meaningful if the underlying detection-and-response pipeline can (i) recognize an adversary's behavior early enough to open a useful window, (ii) remain trustworthy when that behavior is genuinely novel, and (iii) provide evidence that downstream prediction and response modules can reason about under uncertainty.

The EMS is one of the most demanding settings for that pipeline. RF environments are non-stationary, congested, and adversarial by design: jammers, spoofers, replay emitters, and cooperative radios may share spectrum, while the same waveform parameters that characterize a legitimate radio can be perturbed deliberately to evade a learned classifier. Most existing RF classifiers are trained and evaluated under a closed-world assumption where every test sample is assumed to belong to one of the training classes. This assumption fails precisely when it matters most, namely when the adversary deploys a signal behavior the model has never seen. Closed-set softmax probabilities can collapse onto a known class with high confidence regardless of how out-of-distribution the input is, which is the worst possible failure mode in EW: the system reports certainty exactly when the operator should be alerted to ambiguity.

Recent work on confidence-based novelty management for deep neural networks, in particular varMax~\cite{baye2024varmax,broggi2025varmax}, addresses this gap by reshaping the classifier's decision surface so that low-evidence inputs can be flagged as \textit{unknown} rather than silently misrouted to the nearest known class. Trott et al.~\cite{trott2026model} extend this line of work to RF modulation classification and show that signal-domain novelty can be detected without retraining the underlying backbone. Tiwari et al.~\cite{tiwaridqn} further show, in the network intrusion-detection setting, that softmax-derived uncertainty features can support a DQN-based known/unknown decision head. These results suggest that open-set recognition (OSR) can serve as the sensing layer for larger adversarial decision systems.

Detection alone, however, does not produce a QR-CWoS. A classifier that says ``this looks novel'' hands the system a piece of evidence, not a complete course of action. In a Cyber-EMS setting, the gap between recognition and response is itself the window the adversary is trying to close. The defender therefore needs a pipeline in which RF observations become preliminary-action evidence, preliminary-action evidence updates downstream attack-chain or technique hypotheses, and those hypotheses inform response planning. In related work from our research program, MITRE ATT\&CK-based attack-chain prediction uses observed technique prefixes to forecast likely adversary progressions and risk-rank future chains. In parallel, LLM-assisted label-making can analyze detected unknowns, filter remaining false positives, and organize novel observations into candidate new classes. The RF OSR problem studied in this paper supplies the front end for that cycle: it determines whether an OTA signal window belongs to a known preliminary-action class or should be preserved as an unknown behavior for downstream analysis.

A third element specific to EW makes the integration tractable. Many electronic or cyber-physical attacks are preceded by RF-observable preliminary actions (PAs): behaviors such as scanning, bursting, sustained emission, frequency hopping, or short-segment replay that occur before the attack reaches its operational objective. PAs are valuable for two reasons. First, they are earlier than the attack itself, so detecting a PA can open a window before the adversary has committed. Second, the PA taxonomy is behaviorally grounded, which makes it a tractable label space for an open-set classifier and a tractable evidence space for downstream prediction. We do not treat PAs as ATT\&CK technique labels directly. Instead, we treat them as precursor-level evidence: Scan-like behavior can support wireless reconnaissance hypotheses such as Wireless Sniffing; Replay-like behavior can support wireless compromise or unauthorized-command hypotheses; and other PA behaviors can provide RF evidence for EW or ICS-relevant precursor states. The \textit{unknown} output of the classifier on a PA stream is then not just a curiosity. It is the signal that an adversary may be attempting a behavior outside the trained taxonomy, and it is the sample pool from which a continual-learning system can construct future labels.

In this paper, we focus on the RF sensing and OSR layer required by that larger QR-CWoS pipeline. We introduce DQNGuard, a three-stage open-set decision layer that uses predicted-class conditional calibration, variance/energy evidence, and known-only budgeted thresholding to reject samples that do not conform to the learned known-class decision structure. We evaluate DQNGuard on over-the-air RF preliminary-action captures spanning WiFi, Bluetooth, and Zigbee emissions and five behavior classes: Scan, Burst, Sustain, Hop, and Replay. The goal is not to claim that this paper solves end-to-end response selection; rather, it establishes whether the RF front end can reliably identify known and unknown preliminary signal behaviors well enough to support downstream attack-chain prediction, LLM-assisted label-making, and QR-CWoS response planning.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We formulate open-world RF preliminary-action detection as the sensing layer for QR-CWoS: detected PAs provide precursor evidence for downstream ATT\&CK/EW technique prediction and response planning, while detected unknowns provide the sample pool for future label-making.
    \item We introduce DQNGuard, a budgeted OSR decision layer that combines predicted-class conditional calibration, variance/energy guard evidence, and known-only thresholding to control known-class rejection while identifying unknown signal behaviors.
    \item We evaluate DQNGuard on an OTA preliminary-action dataset spanning three protocol families and five PA behaviors, using fixed Scan-surrogate, Target--Surrogate Matrix, and target-open calibration regimes.
    \item We analyze surrogate-open calibration behavior and show that surrogate choice materially affects unknown detection, motivating future work on principled surrogate selection and continual RF behavior discovery.
\end{enumerate}
