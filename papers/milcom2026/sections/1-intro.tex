Modern military operations no longer unfold in a single domain. Adversaries coordinate effects across land, sea, air, space, cyberspace, and the electromagnetic spectrum (EMS), and the boundaries between these domains are increasingly porous in the sense that a cyber intrusion may be enabled by an RF foothold, and an electronic-warfare (EW) action may be the precursor to a kinetic engagement. Sustaining warfighting overmatch in this environment requires more than passive monitoring; it requires the deliberate engineering of Quantifiable and Resilient Cyberspace Windows of Superiority (QR-CWoS). These are bounded intervals during which defenders can act with information and timing advantage over an adversary, and during which that advantage can be measured, predicted, and reproduced. A QR-CWoS is only meaningful if the underlying detection-and-response pipeline can (i) recognize an adversary's behavior early enough to open a useful window, (ii) remain trustworthy when that behavior is genuinely novel, and (iii) commit to a response action whose consequences can be reasoned about under uncertainty.

The EMS is one of the most demanding settings for that pipeline. RF environments are non-stationary, congested, and adversarial by design: jammers, spoofers, and replay emitters share spectrum with cooperative traffic, and the same waveform parameters that characterize a legitimate radio can be perturbed deliberately to evade a learned classifier. Most existing RF classifiers are trained and evaluated under a closed-world assumption where every test sample is assumed to belong to one of the training classes. This assumption fails precisely when it matters most, namely when the adversary deploys a tactic the model has never seen. Closed-set softmax probabilities collapse onto a known class with high confidence regardless of how out-of-distribution the input is, which is the worst possible failure mode in EW: the system reports certainty exactly when the operator should be alerted to ambiguity. 

Recent work on confidence-based novelty management for deep neural networks, in particular varMax \cite{baye2024varmax,broggi2025varmax}, addresses this gap by reshaping the classifier's decision surface so that low-evidence inputs can be flagged as \textit{unknown} rather than silently misrouted to the nearest known class. Trott et al. \cite{trott2026model} extend this line of work to RF modulation classification and show that signal-domain novelty can be detected without retraining the underlying backbone.

Detection alone, however, does not produce a QR-CWoS. A classifier that says ``this looks novel'' hands the operator a label, not a course of action. In EW, the gap between recognition and response is itself the window the adversary is trying to close. The defender therefore needs a policy that maps each classifier output (including the explicit \textit{unknown} verdict and its associated confidence) to an appropriate response: continue passive monitoring, allocate additional sensing, deploy a deception emitter, hand off to a higher-fidelity classifier, or escalate. Tiwari et al. \cite{tiwaridqn} show that a deep Q-network (DQN) can learn such a policy in the open-set intrusion-detection setting, treating the classifier output and its uncertainty as part of the state and learning to act profitably even when the true class label is unavailable. Their framing is naturally compatible with adversarial risk analysis. The DQN's value function encodes the expected payoff of each response under the adversary's likely reaction, which is precisely what QR-CWoS quantification requires.

A third element specific to EW makes the integration tractable. Many electronic attacks are preceded by preliminary actions (PAs). RF-observable behaviors such as bursting, sustained emission, frequency hopping, or short-segment replay that occur before the attack reaches its operational objective \cite{trott2026model}. PAs are valuable for two reasons. First, they are earlier than the attack itself, so detecting a PA opens a window before the adversary has committed. Second, the PA taxonomy is finite and behaviorally grounded, which makes it a tractable label space for an open-set classifier and a tractable state space for a downstream policy. The \textit{unknown} output of the classifier on a PA stream is then not just a curiosity; it is the signal that an adversary is attempting a tactic outside the trained taxonomy, and it is the trigger that the response policy must learn to handle.
In this paper, we connect these three threads. We compare varMax as the open-set recognition layer over an RF preliminary-action classifier, and DQN as the response policy over the (label, confidence, novelty-flag) tuple emitted the pipeline, which is a mechanism for opening, measuring, and sustaining QR-CWoS in contested Cyber-EMS environments.

Our contributions are: (1) An end-to-end open-world RF preliminary-action pipeline that combines varMax-based novelty management \cite{baye2024varmax,broggi2025varmax} with a DQN response policy \cite{tiwaridqn}, evaluated on the PA taxonomy of \cite{trott2026model} (burst, sustain, hop, replay, plus held-out unseen tactics).
(2) A QR-CWoS quantification scheme that maps the temporal gap between PA detection and adversary tactic completion to a measurable window, and that ties the DQN's reward signal to that window's duration and reliability rather than to classification accuracy alone.
(3) An evaluation protocol for unseen-tactic robustness, including held-out PAs that are present in the over-the-air (OTA) test stream but absent at training time, demonstrating that the pipeline degrades gracefully through flagging novelty and selecting conservative responses rather than failing silently.
