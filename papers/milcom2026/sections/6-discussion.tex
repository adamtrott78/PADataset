The results support three main observations.

First, DQNGuard improves open-set preliminary-action recognition under the fixed PA1-surrogate known-budget setting. Table~\ref{tab:main_osr_results} shows that DQNGuard obtains higher unknown F1 and OSR macro F1 than both the VarMax surrogate-all baseline and the Shreyash CNN head comparison while maintaining a low known-rejection rate. This distinction is important because high unknown detection can be obtained trivially by rejecting too many known samples. DQNGuard instead uses known-only budgeted thresholding to constrain the allowable known rejection, making its gains more relevant for operational sensing pipelines where known preliminary-action evidence must remain usable.

Second, surrogate-open calibration is useful but not interchangeable across preliminary actions. Fig.~\ref{fig:target_surrogate_matrix} shows large variation across target--surrogate pairs. Some target unknowns are detected well under several surrogate choices, while others depend strongly on the surrogate used for calibration. This behavior suggests that the surrogate class does not merely provide generic ``unknownness'' evidence. Rather, it induces a calibration geometry determined by how the withheld surrogate behavior differs from the remaining known behaviors. Consequently, a surrogate that is useful for one target unknown may be uninformative or misleading for another.

Third, these findings clarify the role of the RF front end in a larger QR-CWoS pipeline. The proposed system does not select a response action, infer a complete attack chain, or assign final semantic labels to all novel emissions. Instead, it produces two evidence streams: known preliminary-action evidence for recognized behaviors and an unknown pool for nonconforming behaviors. The known stream can support precursor-level ATT\&CK/EW hypothesis generation, while the unknown stream can feed LLM-assisted label-making and continual-learning workflows. In this sense, DQNGuard is best interpreted as a sensing and triage layer that expands the amount of usable evidence available before a downstream response planner acts.

The main limitation is that surrogate selection remains unresolved. The Target--Surrogate Matrix demonstrates that some surrogates transfer better than others, but the route-alignment and pseudo-target selection diagnostics evaluated so far do not reliably predict the best surrogate across folds. This limits the current method's ability to choose surrogate-open calibration classes automatically. A practical deployment would therefore require either a conservative fixed-surrogate policy, multiple surrogate diagnostics with human review, or a more principled surrogate-selection rule trained across a larger family of preliminary actions.

A second limitation is scale. The evaluated corpus covers three protocol families and five preliminary-action behaviors, but the target--surrogate analysis is necessarily limited by the number of available behavior classes. Broader RF behavior taxonomies would make it possible to evaluate pooled surrogate calibration, leave-multiple-out calibration, and learned surrogate selection more rigorously. Such experiments would also help determine whether the observed transfer asymmetries are specific to the current action set or reflect a more general property of RF behavior novelty detection.

Finally, the current paper evaluates open-set recognition after window extraction and feature construction. End-to-end operational performance will also depend on upstream burst detection, segmentation, synchronization, and OTA capture quality, as well as downstream label-making and response-policy modules. These dependencies do not weaken the OSR result, but they define the boundary of the claim: DQNGuard shows that unknown preliminary-action behaviors can be detected from OTA RF windows under controlled calibration regimes, not that a complete autonomous EW response loop has been solved.
